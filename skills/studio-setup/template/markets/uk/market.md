# Market pack: United Kingdom (default)

Who the videos are for, and everything that changes because of it. The visual style, pipeline
and components stay the same (see `reference/channel-formula.md`); this file decides the
language, voice, sources, settings, hooks and legal care.

## Audience

British viewers, 18–45, scrolling Reels/Shorts/TikTok. They know the high street, supermarkets,
corner shops, school tuck shops and 90s/00s telly. They respond to nostalgia ("I remember
that!"), a business twist they didn't know, and a dry, understated tone. Don't explain what
they already know (what Woolworths was); explain what they don't (why it went under).

## Language

- British English spelling and words: colour, flavour, favourite, centre, programme, realise,
  organise, cheque, aluminium, tyre, mum, crisps (not chips), chips (not fries), sweets (not
  candy), biscuits (not cookies), fizzy drinks, shop/high street (not store/mall), car park,
  queue, holiday, autumn, telly, quid (sparingly), pocket money.
- Money: £1.99, 99p, £2.3 million, £1.2bn. Pre-1971 prices in shillings and pence only with the
  decimal equivalent. Say "pounds" and "pence" in narration, not "dollars" or "cents".
- Dates: 12 March 1998 (titles: "1998", "March 1998"). Decades: "the 90s".
- Units: miles, pints for drinks; metric for weights unless historical.
- Tone: warm, wry, understated; light humour is fine, no hype words ("insane", "crazy").
  Avoid Americanisms ("awesome", "gotten", "math", "fall" for autumn).

## Voice

Calm British narrator, neutral Southern English or soft regional accent, warm documentary
storyteller, about 140–150 wpm. Defaults live in `markets/uk/voice.json`; change the voice there.

## Hooks that work

- "If you grew up in the 90s, you'll remember…"
- "Every town had one. Then, almost overnight, they were gone."
- "You've probably bought one this week. But it started in a…"
- "This was once the most popular sweet in Britain. So where did it go?"
- A price then and now ("In 1985 this cost 10p…").

## Arcs: what UK stories usually turn on

Decline: rise on the high street → competition from supermarkets / out-of-town retail parks /
Amazon / discounters (Aldi, Lidl) → debt, private-equity buyouts, rent and business rates →
**administration** → stores close → twist: brand bought out of administration and now online
only / revived / owned abroad / still made somewhere.

Success: started at a market stall, a kitchen table, a festival, a garage or a uni bedroom →
a clever idea or bit of marketing → growth → listed on the stock market / bought / national
treasure → today.

Renames are a reliable hook: Marathon → Snickers, Opal Fruits → Starburst, Jif → Cif,
Dime → Daim.

## Research sources (UK)

- **Companies House** (filing history, accounts, administrators' and liquidators' reports,
  officers): primary evidence for dates, owners, administrations and "still trading today".
- **The Gazette** (official notices of insolvency).
- **Press**: BBC News, The Guardian, The Times, Financial Times, The Telegraph, Sky News, Reuters;
  regional papers for origin stories.
- **Trade press**: The Grocer, Retail Gazette, Drapers, Campaign, Marketing Week, Music Week.
- **Data**: Kantar, NielsenIQ, Mintel and ONS figures, cited as reported by the press.
- **Brand archives and adverts**: company history pages, archived ads (cite, don't copy).
- Prefer two independent sources for anything about why a business failed.

## Legal care (UK)

- **Defamation**: UK law is claimant-friendly. Stick to documented facts; attribute opinions
  and causes ("according to the administrators", "analysts blamed…"); never imply wrongdoing by
  a named living person without a reliable published source; no speculation about fraud.
  Rumours appear only as rumours, attributed and clearly unverified.
- **Trademarks and logos**: draw products as generic, recognisable shapes and colours with
  the name as plain text where needed; don't copy logos, mascots or packaging artwork exactly.
- **Real people**: no caricatures of identifiable living people (founders, celebrities in
  adverts); use generic figures with a name caption.
- **Copyright**: no clips of TV adverts, programmes or music; describe and redraw.

## Settings and props (draw these instead of the reference videos' settings)

High street with shop fronts and hanging signs; corner shop / newsagent counter with sweet jars,
newspapers and a lottery stand; supermarket aisle and trolley; retail park and car park;
terraced houses and a red-brick street; school tuck shop and playground; living room with a
CRT telly; seaside pier; market stall; pub frontage; rain clouds; a double-decker bus or black
cab only when it helps place the scene (avoid postcard clichés).

Characters: everyday Britain across ages and backgrounds; school uniforms (blazer, jumper, tie),
office workers, shop assistants in tabards, mums and dads with prams, pensioners. Clothing by
decade when the story moves in time (70s flares, 90s shell suits and bucket hats, 00s hoodies).

UK metaphors to add to the library:

| Idea | Metaphor |
|---|---|
| Administration / closing down | "CLOSING DOWN" and "EVERYTHING MUST GO" banners, shutters coming down |
| The high street dying | a row of shops where lights go out one by one; "TO LET" boards |
| Out-of-town competition | a car with a trolley driving out of town to a giant retail-park box |
| Online took over | a parcel van replacing the shop front; a laptop with a delivery box |
| Debt | a stack of letters marked "FINAL DEMAND"; a weight chained to the shop |
| Bought out of administration | the brand sign lifted off the shop by a crane onto a laptop screen |
| Rename | old wrapper peeled off to show the new one |
| Price then and now | coin stack shrinking/growing next to a price tag |
| National favourite | a Union Jack bunting row over the product (sparingly) |

## Titles and on-screen text

British spelling; £ before amounts; "p" for pence ("10p"); keep brand names as plain text in
the title font, never as logo artwork.
